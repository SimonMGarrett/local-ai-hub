# Reader comprehension and worked-example assessment

## Purpose

Assess whether the current website makes the MOSAIC proposition clear and convincing, beyond reading age alone, and whether the worked examples accurately teach MOSAIC principles.

## Evidence status

This document began as an expert review of the live website and source as at 15 September 2026. Initial evidence from one first-time reader was added on 24 September 2026. One participant is a useful directional signal, not a completed study or sufficient evidence for a redesign.

No completed multi-participant reader-comprehension study or runnable research protocol was found in the repository. The existing website specification contains six success questions, but does not define the participant journey, acceptable answers, evidence capture, or how to distinguish understanding from repetition.

## Overall finding

The site communicates the central problem well: MOSAIC supports decisions when evidence is incomplete or fragmented. It explains MOSAIC values such as traceability, visible uncertainty, alternatives, and defensibility more strongly than it explains the practical mechanism.

A first-time reader may still be unclear about:

* who should use MOSAIC and in which situations;
* what someone actually does when applying it;
* the tangible output, especially the decision record;
* why the method is more useful than ordinary good decision-making;
* how the six stages work together iteratively.

## Readability

A rough Flesch-based calculation on the live text produced approximate US grade levels of:

* Homepage: grade 12
* Framework: grade 13
* Worked-examples index: grade 10
* Meaning + Observations: grade 10
* Structure + Analysis: grade 15
* Interpretation + Choice: grade 13

These are directional heuristics, especially on pages containing interface labels. The pattern nevertheless indicates a relatively demanding reading level. Terms such as provenance, corroboration, possibilistic, decision-sensitive, ordinal comparison, and supportable choice increase the burden.

The homepage contains about 750 words and the framework about 1,060. At an ordinary reading pace these take approximately four and five minutes respectively, before a reader tries an example. The five-minute success condition therefore needs a defined route and exposure.

## Proposition copy

### What works

* "Make strong decisions from fragmented evidence" is concise and memorable.
* The analysis to decision to action model gives MOSAIC a recognisable place in a workflow.
* The copy consistently avoids promising certainty.
* "A decision record that can be revisited" is the most tangible expression of the output.
* The distinction between collecting more information and deciding what could change the choice is compelling.

### What weakens comprehension

* The hero does not identify the intended user or a recognisable moment when MOSAIC should be used.
* Reasoned, defensible, traceable, and supportable recur without immediately demonstrating what makes the resulting decision better.
* The tangible output, a decision record, appears relatively late.
* "Analysis can establish what is true" is stronger than the rest of the framework supports; elsewhere the site more accurately says what may be true.
* The framework page is thorough but functions more as reference material than as a first-time explanation.
* There is no single plain-language sentence describing the end-to-end movement through the six stages.

A candidate proposition for testing is:

"When an important decision cannot wait for perfect information, MOSAIC helps you define the choice, organise the evidence, test competing interpretations, and record why a particular action is justified — and what might change it."

## Worked-example assessment

### Meaning + Observations

Partly successful. Deadline and reversibility clearly change the decision framing, and evidence types remain visible. However, adding a rumour or reported judgement changes only the item count; it does not visibly affect relevance or subsequent reasoning. The claimed lesson is stronger than the demonstrated behaviour.

### Structure + Analysis

The strongest individual teaching example. Linking duplicate reports makes source dependence tangible. However, when all three explanations have one independent signal, the interface still declares the release explanation the analytical lead. This arbitrary tie-break undermines the lesson about disciplined analysis.

### Interpretation + Choice

Strong. It demonstrates that unchanged findings can support different choices when consequences and constraints change. Residual uncertainty and reconsideration triggers reinforce MOSAIC well. The label "assumptions" is loose: supplier qualification is closer to an unresolved fact, while switching cost is a constraint.

### Complete decision

The weakest representation of MOSAIC despite being the most ambitious. The live record and six-stage navigation are useful, but the outcome comes from an undisclosed point calculation. Evidence is reduced to supporting accept or stay; provenance, dependence, contradictions, alternative explanations, gaps, and rejected choices largely disappear. This risks teaching that MOSAIC is a weighted scoring calculator.

Across the examples, iteration and targeted inquiry are not sufficiently demonstrated.

## Assessment of the existing success questions

The existing six questions are useful intent but not a runnable comprehension test.

* Asking for the major stages tests recall more than understanding.
* Asking what one experiment reveals permits a vague answer and tests only one example.
* Asking why Simon might be useful to speak with measures credibility or conversion rather than MOSAIC comprehension.
* The questions do not test intended user, tangible output, iteration, decision ownership, or when more evidence is worthwhile.
* They do not require transfer of a principle to a new situation.

## Recommended reader test

Use a short moderated session with representatives of the intended audiences. Do not explain MOSAIC beforehand.

### 1. First impression

Briefly show the opening section, then hide it. Ask:

* What do you think MOSAIC is?
* When might someone use it?
* What would they have at the end that they did not have before?

### 2. Five-minute exploration

Allow free navigation. Ask the participant to explain MOSAIC to a colleague without looking at the site.

Listen for decision context, incomplete evidence, alternatives or uncertainty, a justified action, and conditions for reconsideration. Do not require exact stage names.

### 3. Worked-example comprehension

Give the participant one paired example. Before changing a control, ask what they expect to happen and why. Afterwards ask:

* What changed?
* Why did it change?
* Which MOSAIC principle did that demonstrate?
* Where might the same principle apply in a different situation?

### 4. Method integrity

Ask:

* Is MOSAIC making the decision or helping a person make it?
* How is it different from gathering more data?
* What should happen when a critical gap or weak assumption is discovered?

## Evidence to retain

Record verbatim answers, navigation choices, hesitation, unfamiliar terms, and confident misunderstandings. Treat comprehension as demonstrated only when a reader can explain causality and transfer the principle, not when they repeat wording from the page.

## Initial reader evidence — participant 1 — 24 September 2026

### Session context

The participant explored the MOSAIC website and was then asked to give a short presentation back to Simon explaining what she understood. The available account does not include timed navigation, verbatim answers beyond the quotations below, participant background, or a full record against every question in the proposed protocol.

### Observed

* She understood the overall MOSAIC concept without much difficulty.
* In the presentation-back exercise, she recovered most of the six stages. This suggests that the framework itself was substantially comprehensible to this participant.
* The **Meaning + Observations** example made the most sense to her.
* She described both **Structure + Analysis** and **Interpretation + Choice** as “too wordy”.
* When a proposition changed in those paired examples, she had to “read a lot to see what had changed”.
* The complete worked example was better than the two wordier paired examples.
* The complete example nevertheless constrained her response. She said: “I had to choose from a list that had already been decided.”
* She wanted to say things that the available choices did not permit.

### Interpretation

These observations separate two problems that should not be conflated:

1. **Framework comprehension:** this participant could understand and recount most of MOSAIC.
2. **Worked-example interaction quality:** several examples imposed excessive comparison effort or insufficient freedom of expression.

The paired examples may be making the reader rediscover changes through repeated reading rather than making the consequential difference visible. The complete example may demonstrate a predetermined decision path more than it demonstrates how MOSAIC helps a person structure their own judgement.

“Total freedom” is not yet established as the correct solution. Useful possibilities to test later include:

* visibly highlighting what changed and why it matters;
* shortening propositions and using progressive disclosure;
* preserving the structured choices while allowing a participant to add, amend or reject an option;
* accepting free-form input and asking the user to confirm how MOSAIC has interpreted or structured it;
* showing which parts came from the user and which were suggested by the system.

These are hypotheses, not approved design changes.

### Follow-up in the remaining reader sessions

* Check whether other participants can explain the framework even when they do not remember exact stage names.
* Ask participants to identify what changed in each paired example and observe how much rereading is required.
* Ask what they wanted to enter or change when the available choices felt restrictive.
* Test whether visible difference-highlighting improves understanding without adding explanation.
* Distinguish a desire for different predefined options from a need for genuinely free-form input.
* Retain the participant’s own proposed response so it can inform a later interaction prototype.
