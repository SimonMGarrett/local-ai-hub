## Glossary
These are plain-language paraphrases, not new canonical terminology.
- Meaning: defining the decision and what matters. [S4/C1]
- Observations: recording available material and what kind of knowledge it is. [S4/C2]
- Structure: retaining the links among sources, claims, assumptions and choices. [S4/C3]
- Analysis: examining what evidence supports, challenges or leaves unresolved. [S4/C4]
- Interpretation: explaining what findings imply for the possible choices. [S4/C5]
- Choice: committing with reasons, remaining uncertainty and reconsideration conditions. [S4/C6]
- Provenance: where an item came from and the context needed to assess it. [S4, Observations/Structure]
- Assumption: something being relied on without treating it as an established observation; test its influence on the decision. [S4, Observations/Analysis; S9]
- Corroboration: support from evidence; shared-origin reports cannot automatically be treated as independent support. [S4, Analysis; S7]
- Contradiction: material pointing against another claim or explanation, which remains visible rather than being edited away. [S4, Structure/Analysis]
- Decision-sensitive uncertainty: an unresolved matter whose resolution could alter the supportable choice. [S4, Analysis/Interpretation/Choice]
- Decision record: an inspectable account linking a choice with evidence, assumptions and rationale. A storage format is not prescribed by this baseline. [S3/S4; final sentence is inference I1]
- Reconsideration condition: new evidence or a changed assumption that should prompt review of a prior choice. [S4, Choice; S8/S9]

## Archived worked example: a reversible onboarding change
**Founder disposition (S10, 2026-09-27): not endorsed as a useful teaching application.** None of the current teaching examples should be taken as entirely useful. They may be ignored or referred to loosely; refining examples for the six stages remains future work. The draft below is preserved to make the reviewed material inspectable, not to recommend its use.
**Epistemic status:** new synthetic teaching example, proposed by The Oracle. Not a historical case, measured result, or official founder example. It adapts the product/onboarding patterns in S4 and the duplicate-source lesson in S7. All dates, quantities, costs and permissions below are hypothetical.

**Assumptions:** a product lead may authorize a £500, two-day reversible interface change. Pausing the product would require a different approval. No safety-critical functionality is involved. The team has two days to decide.

1. **Meaning (C1).** Decide whether to fix onboarding, investigate measurement first, leave the interface alone, or seek approval to pause the product. The desired outcome is successful onboarding; the lead must respect the hypothetical cost and time limits.
2. **Observations (C2).** E1: a dashboard shows completion falling from 60% to 45% after release; its event definition changed on release day. E2: the weekly email repeats that dashboard. E3: in four of six hypothetical observed sessions, users cannot find the next-action button; these sessions use a separate observation record. E4: no demand study is available. A1: moving the button is reversible within a day. A2: those six sessions may reveal a problem relevant beyond those participants, but representativeness is unproven.
3. **Structure (C3).** Link E2 to E1's originating data so they count as one provenance chain. Connect the tracking change to uncertainty about the apparent decline. Connect E3 to the usability explanation, while preserving its small-sample limitation. Link A1 to the reversible-fix option. Preserve the alternatives that instrumentation, usability, demand changes, or a mixture explain the apparent decline.
4. **Analysis (C4).** E1/E2 cannot establish a comparable pre/post decline without checking the changed definition. E3 supports a local usability problem in those sessions; it does not prove population prevalence or overall causality. E4 leaves weak demand unresolved, not demonstrated. Six sessions are not converted into a calibrated probability.
5. **Interpretation (C5).** If A1 is true, the limited usability evidence can support a small reversible improvement while measurement is checked. It does not establish grounds for the much larger step of pausing the product. The distinction rests on the stipulated consequences and reversibility, not an invented confidence score.
6. **Choice (C6).** In this hypothetical scenario, authorize the limited fix within £500, assign an analyst to check event comparability, and record why immediate pause and unchanged onboarding were not selected. Owner: product lead. Review after two days using comparable measurement and new observed sessions. Reconsider immediately if rollback proves unavailable, the cost cap is exceeded, or evidence shows harm to completion.

**Sensitivity test:** if A1 is false and rollback requires a month, the justification for immediate rollout weakens. First investigate or use a safely bounded test subject to the lead's actual authority. If comparable measurements show no decline, E3 can still justify addressing observed friction, but claims about a general drop must be withdrawn. These are conditional teaching judgements, not automatic MOSAIC rules.

**Traceability check:** the dashboard supports only the reported metric; sessions support only observed friction; the choice relies explicitly on A1, authority and cost limits. The example does not claim empirical validation. It follows C1–C6, preserves provenance under C8, tests information value under C9, and links action to consequence under C10.

## FAQ
**What is MOSAIC?** A developing framework for making inspectable, defensible choices from imperfect evidence. C1–C13 are confirmed as the current baseline bounded to the inspected website; this is not empirical validation. [S3–S5, S10]
**Must I complete six stages exactly once?** No. The site describes iterative forms of reasoning, even though it numbers stages. [S3/S4]
**Does MOSAIC replace analysis?** The published model takes analytical material into decision reasoning; it also includes Analysis internally. The founder confirmed that external analysis supplies material while MOSAIC Analysis examines structured evidence for this decision. [S4, S10]
**Does it make the decision for me?** Its published aim is to support structured judgement. The complete example leaves commitment with the decision owner. [S3/S9]
**Does more evidence always improve the decision?** The published Choice questions ask whether attainable information could change the choice and warrant its cost. [S4]
**Do example scores represent probabilities?** S9 explicitly identifies its comparison as ordinal, not probabilistic. This baseline finds no universal scoring specification. [S9; inventory limitation]
**Can we call it proven?** Not from these sources. They explain and demonstrate; no efficacy study was found in the inspected set. [source register]
**Has the website met its comprehension goal?** No result is available here. The plan's target is at least eight of the same ten people both explaining it and scoring at least four of five on a quiz. Preparing material does not demonstrate that outcome. [S1/S2]

## Archived understanding checks (deferred proposal, not the website study)
These checks depend on the unendorsed example and are not an approved teaching or evaluation instrument. [S10]
Ask a reader to explain why E1 and E2 are not two independent confirmations; identify the assumption carrying the reversible-fix decision; and name evidence that would change the choice.
Expected answers: common dashboard origin; rollback/authority/cost assumptions, especially A1; comparable metrics, adverse observed effects, or loss of reversibility. A useful explanation connects evidence to the consequence of acting; recalling stage names alone is insufficient. Chief of Staff owns the formal quiz, rubric and test protocol.
